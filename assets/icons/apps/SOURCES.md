# The app marks

Twenty-eight brand marks from Simple Icons v15.16.0, fetched from
jsDelivr and rasterised to 256px PNG, black on transparent, so they
behave like the kit and pick up the same `iconFilter` on a dark ground.

## The licence, and the part the licence does not cover

The Simple Icons **files** are CC0-1.0: public domain, no attribution
required. That is the easy half.

The hard half is that CC0 covers the drawing and not the trademark. Each
mark is still the property of the company it names, and Simple Icons say
so themselves: they ask users to read each brand's own guidelines and to
seek permission where it is needed, and they disclaim responsibility for
what anybody does with the files.

Two of the marks I went looking for are already gone from the set:
`linkedin` and `adobe` both 404. Simple Icons removes a mark when the
brand asks them to, which is the clearest possible demonstration that
CC0 on the file is not permission from the brand.

## The rule, then

A mark may only **name a product the slide is talking about**. That is
nominative use and it is what these are for: a slide about what Search
Console tells you may show the Search Console mark.

It may not:

- sit anywhere a logo sits — the rail, the corner, beside `@web3ashley`
- appear on a slide that does not discuss that product
- be recoloured into the palette, stretched, rotated, or drawn on
- sit next to a claim the brand has not made, or in any arrangement
  that reads as "these people use me" or "I work with these people"

They ship monochrome and they stay monochrome. That is how Simple Icons
distributes them and it is also the treatment least likely to be taken
for the brand's own asset.

If a brand's guidelines say something stricter than the above, the
guidelines win. Look them up before using a mark on a post that goes
out, rather than after.
